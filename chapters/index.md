A quadrotor's attitude is a rotation. A cable's direction is a point on a sphere. Neither is a list of numbers you can subtract, average or linearize the way you would a position, and most of the trouble in attitude control comes from pretending otherwise.

This book treats the configuration space as what it is: a curved manifold, usually built from $\SO(3)$, $S^2$ and $\R^3$. On that footing it develops modeling, control, estimation and trajectory optimization for robots, with one notation from the first page to the last.

Every chapter is built the same way. It opens with a concrete puzzle. It develops the idea through thought experiments and worked examples before the general statement. It ends with exercises, a notebook you can run, and a script that checks every derivation in the chapter numerically.

The reader we have in mind has taken linear algebra, a first course in dynamics and a first course in control. No differential geometry is assumed. The geometry is introduced when a robot needs it.
