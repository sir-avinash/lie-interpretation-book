A Manifold Introduction to Robotics and Control

Coordinate-free modeling, control, estimation and optimization

Download the book as PDF · Read as a single page · Source on GitHub

A quadrotor’s attitude is a rotation. A cable’s direction is a point on a sphere. Neither is a list of numbers you can subtract, average or linearize the way you would a position, and most of the trouble in attitude control comes from pretending otherwise.

This book treats the configuration space as what it is: a curved manifold, usually built from SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3), S2S^2S2 and R3\R^3R3. On that footing it develops modeling, control, estimation and trajectory optimization for robots, with one notation from the first page to the last.

Every chapter is built the same way. It opens with a concrete puzzle. It develops the idea through thought experiments and worked examples before the general statement. It ends with exercises, a notebook you can run, and a script that checks every derivation in the chapter numerically.

The reader we have in mind has taken linear algebra, a first course in dynamics and a first course in control. No differential geometry is assumed. The geometry is introduced when a robot needs it.

Contents

I. Foundations

  1. 1Why coordinate-free?

    Euler angles break, quaternions unwind, and no continuous controller can be global. What to do about it.

  2. 2The manifolds robots live on

    Circles, spheres, rotations and rigid motions. What a tangent vector is, why rotations form a group and directions do not, and how to take a time step without falling off.

  3. 3Variations, errors and transport

    How to nudge a configuration, measure how far off it is, and compare two velocities that live in different places.

II. Modeling

  1. 4Dynamics without coordinates

    Hamilton’s principle carried out on the sphere and on the rotation group, and the equations of motion of pendulums, rigid bodies, quadrotors and cable-suspended loads that come out of it.

  2. 5Symbolic derivation

    The variational recipe is term rewriting, so a program can run it on the manifold. How it works, how to check it, and how to turn its output into a simulation.

III. Geometric control

  1. 6Tracking control and almost-global stability

    A proportional-derivative law built from the errors of Chapter 3, a Lyapunov proof you can check line by line, and an honest account of what “from almost anywhere” means for rigid bodies, directions, quadrotors and legs.

  2. 7Variation-based linearization and LQR

    What a small deviation of a rotation is, how to linearize about any trajectory without a singularity, and how far the resulting LQR can be trusted.

  3. 8Robust, adaptive and safety-critical control

    A tracking controller assumes a perfect model and knows nothing of walls. An adaptive loop on the manifold supplies the first, a barrier function the second.

IV. Reduction

  1. 9Symmetry, fiber bundles and connectionsplanned
  2. 10Differential flatness

    Draw a smooth curve for the load and a heading, and the whole state and all four inputs follow by algebra and differentiation. Why that works, how many derivatives it costs, and where it breaks.

  3. 11Hybrid zero dynamics on manifoldsplanned

V. Trajectory optimization

  1. 12Variational integrators and collocation

    Discretize Hamilton’s principle instead of the equations of motion, and the integrator keeps the momentum, the manifold and, very nearly, the energy. The same discrete equations serve as constraints for trajectory optimization.

  2. 13Error-state MPC and on-manifold solvers

    What an optimizer’s variables should be when the state is a rotation, how tracking on a group becomes a quadratic program, and how Newton and interior-point methods work on the manifold itself.

VI. Estimation and learning

  1. 14Filtering on Lie groups

    An estimation error whose dynamics do not depend on the estimate, the invariant extended Kalman filter built on it, and what to do when the state is a sphere and not a group.

VII. Case studies

  1. 16How it all comes together

    Every robot in the book is the same few factors assembled the same way, and every technique applies to each by one recipe. A library of models, and a map of where each is treated.
